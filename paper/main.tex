\begin{abstract}
Clinical agents read records, order tests and write notes, and every step they take is a decision generated by a large
language model (LLM) after thousands of tokens of reasoning. System One decision models decide in a single forward
pass: they read a state and a set of options and return a probability for each. We put six such models in the
decision seat of a real clinical agent loop. On PhysicianBench, 100 consultation tasks on an electronic health record
of real de-identified patients, the role matters more than the model. Choosing among five turns proposed by the LLM,
a System One model is safe: Clef-27B passes \PBcpSel\% of checkpoints against \PBcpLLM\%, with no significant change in
task success. Choosing the tool itself, it breaks the agent (\PBpassTool\% of tasks pass). Neither role improves on
the LLM, because deciding is not where the LLM's errors are: 79\% of its step-level ``omissions'' of a required order
are placed later anyway, and only 11\% of required orders are ever proposed but not executed, the most any selector
can recover. A decider that recovers part of this loses as much by acting before the LLM has proposed the rest; an
early gain on orders failed a pre-registered replication. What the System One models bring instead is a shared
\emph{commitment bias}: all six prefer turns that order (1.3--1.6$\times$ the LLM's rate) or finish
(1.3--1.4$\times$) over turns that gather information. A pre-registered blind test splits it in two: about two thirds
of the gain on orders needs no patient state, a prior over the options, while the excess of premature finishing
appears only when the model reads the state. We map where System One deciders are reliable (their confidence is
informative; they help where the LLM's samples disagree; finishing is their blind spot), show that step-level answer
keys measure decision style rather than outcome, and derive a division of labour in which the System One model
decides only at order steps.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A physician's agent takes dozens of steps per task: look up the problem list, pull the labs, read last month's note,
order the imaging, write the assessment. Each step is a choice among a small set of concrete actions, and in today's
systems an LLM makes every one of them by generating text \citep{physicianbench2026,yao2023react}.
Figure~\ref{fig:case} follows one such task.

\emph{System One} decision models offer a different way to choose \citep{typesafe2026jev,clef2026}. They take a
state and a typed question with named options and return a probability for every option in one forward pass, with no
generated text and nothing to parse. They are fast, cheap and suited to choosing among options, the operation an
agent performs at every step. Whether they can make an agent's decisions is open. So far they have been studied on
single-shot tasks and as judges \citep{li2026jevjudge}, and in general-purpose agent loops their actions often go wrong
even when they read the state correctly \citep{li2026reading}. No study has placed one in the decision seat of a
clinical agent.

We do so under a strict comparison. Clef-27B is post-trained from Qwen3.8-27B, which serves as our LLM, so the two
decision makers share a backbone and differ in how they read out a decision. We run PhysicianBench in its own harness,
with its own tools and graders, and change only the component that picks the next turn (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).
Five further open System One models take the same seat on a fixed exam of logged decisions, and one of them online.
We organise the study around three questions: how System One models perform in the loop, where their limits lie, and
how they should share the work with the reasoning LLM. The short answer: they are safe as selectors and harmful as
steerers, they cannot do much better than the LLM because the LLM's errors are mostly not in the choice of the next
turn, and what they bring instead is a shared bias toward committing, part prior and part misreading of the state,
which tells us where to use them.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The role matters more than the model} (\S\ref{sec:select}, \S\ref{sec:steer}). Choosing among
  proposals, Clef matches the LLM on checkpoints (\PRSelLLMcp{} points) and neither System One model changes task
  success significantly; choosing the tool, task success falls to \PBpassTool\%.
  \item \textbf{Deciding is not the bottleneck} (\S\ref{sec:timing}). 79\% of the LLM's step-level omissions of a
  required order are placed later in the same run. Of all required orders, 11\% are proposed but never executed, the
  most any selector can recover, and 19\% are never proposed. pplx-decider-v1.2 recovers part of the 11\% and loses
  as much on generation; its early +13 points on required orders failed a pre-registered replication on 24 held-out
  tasks ($-$18 points, 95\% CI $-$36 to $-$2).
  \item \textbf{They carry a commitment bias} (\S\ref{sec:commit}). All six models prefer turns that order or finish
  over turns that gather information. A rule with no model that always takes an ordering turn when one is offered
  reaches most of their advantage on the order key; their apparent avoidance of redundant steps is mostly finishing
  instead of rewriting the note. Asked blind, without the patient state, pplx-decider-v1.2 keeps about two thirds of
  its gain on orders but finishes early less often than chance instead of more: the order preference is largely a
  prior, the excess of premature finishing comes from reading the state.
  \item \textbf{Steering alone, the bias breaks the agent} (\S\ref{sec:steer}). Choosing the tool, Clef cuts
  retrieval before the first action from \TLFirstLLM{} to \TLFirstTool{} calls and writes the note \TLDocTool{}
  times per task.
  \item \textbf{Offline exams measure style} (\S\ref{sec:timing}). Every decider beats the LLM's own sample on a
  fixed exam of logged decisions (0.17--0.27 against 0.48 key errors), but the step-level key rewards ordering early,
  and the exam holds too few consequential decisions to rank deciders on outcome.
  \item \textbf{Where a System One decider is reliable} (\S\ref{sec:boundaries}). Its confidence is informative; it
  helps where the LLM's five samples disagree and adds nothing where they agree; it is confidently wrong about when
  to finish.
  \item \textbf{A narrower division of labour} (\S\ref{sec:gate}). Consulting the System One model only where a
  proposal places an order keeps the decider where its prior helps and away from stopping, and overrules the LLM on
  11\% of steps instead of 73\%. Its pre-registered online test is under way.
\end{itemize}

\section{Putting a System One model in the decision seat}\label{sec:protocol}

An agent loop alternates between a state $s_t$, the task plus every action and result so far, and the next turn
$a_t$: a set of tool calls with arguments, or a final answer. The benchmark samples $a_t \sim \pi(\cdot\mid s_t)$ from
the LLM. We replace only this step.

\paragraph{\llm{} (reference).} One sample from $\pi$ with thinking on and the model's default sampling, exactly as
the benchmark prescribes. A logging variant samples $K{=}5$ turns and executes the first, which has the same
distribution as \llm{} and gives the run-to-run noise floor.

\paragraph{\sel{}: the System One model chooses the turn.} The LLM samples $K{=}5$ turns $a_t^{(1)},\dots,a_t^{(5)}$
i.i.d.\ from $\pi$ in one request. Exact duplicates are merged. The System One model reads the state and the distinct
turns, rendered as tool calls with full arguments, and returns a probability for each; the most probable turn is
executed. It never sees the LLM's reasoning, so it judges actions, not rationales. A uniform pick among $K$ i.i.d.\
samples has the same distribution as \llm, so any difference between the two comes from \emph{which} sample is chosen.

\paragraph{\tool{}: the System One model chooses the kind of action.} The System One model reads the state and the
full tool menu (each tool's name and description, plus \textsc{finish}) and picks one. The LLM then writes the turn
with its calls constrained to the chosen tool. The System One model now sets the agent's strategy and timing, and the
LLM supplies the content. \ctl{} is the control: the same one-tool-per-turn restriction with the LLM choosing (it keeps
only the calls of its own turn's first tool).

\paragraph{Gated selection: System One plus the reasoning LLM.} The System One model is consulted only at some steps
and only overrules the LLM's own first sample there. In the variant we test online it is consulted only where at
least one proposal places a service or medication order; elsewhere, including every choice between finishing,
writing the note and gathering more data, the LLM's first sample is executed. A margin variant overrules only when the
System One model prefers its pick to the LLM's sample by a log-odds margin.

\paragraph{Fair inputs.} Options are named by letters shuffled with a seed per task, run and step. The System One model
reads a deterministic 32k-token view of the state (oldest tool results shortened first); the LLM reads up to 128k
tokens. If the System One model fails, the first sample is executed; this happened \DECSelerr{} times in the
\sel{} run.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{PhysicianBench} \citep{physicianbench2026} adapts 100 real outpatient consultations from 21 specialties
into long-horizon tasks on a FHIR server with the records of 108 de-identified patients (47k conditions, 114k
observations, 35k procedures, 13k medication requests, 2.3k clinical notes). Each task has 5--9 checkpoints (670 in
total) of four types: data retrieval (DR), clinical reasoning (CR), action execution (AE: orders, prescriptions,
referrals placed in the record and verified by code) and documentation (Doc). A task passes only if all of its
checkpoints pass. We use the released harness: a fresh server and database per task, the MiniAgent loop with parallel
tool calls and at most 100 steps, and the task's pytest graders. For analysis we split AE into the 92 code-graded
required-order checkpoints in 54 tasks (the graders' own name and code patterns), and the rest. Only one checkpoint in
100 tasks penalises an order (``must not be ordered''), so the benchmark barely penalises over-ordering.

\paragraph{Models.} The LLM is Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen38} in bf16 with 128k context and thinking on, served by vLLM
\citep{kwon2023vllm}. The main System One model is Clef-27B \citep{clef2026} with its native joint head. The other
five are pplx-decider-v1.2, d3, linne-oak-1.0, amos-1-large and decider-31b, each served by its authors' code at a
pinned revision (Appendix~\ref{app:interface}). The judge is the same Qwen model with thinking off, shared by all arms.
Everything runs on one node with four H100 GPUs; no record leaves it (\S\ref{sec:ethics}).

\paragraph{The fixed exam.} To compare deciders on identical inputs, the LLM runs every task once and logs, at each
step, its five proposals and the full state. Each step with at least two distinct proposals is a question
(\EXquestions{} questions from \EXtasks{} tasks), and every decider answers byte-identical requests: the same state,
the same options, the same option letters. Three keys apply where they separate the options: \emph{order} (the option
places a required order not yet placed), \emph{finish} (finishing while a required order is still open is premature)
and \emph{redundancy} (the option repeats executed calls, or rewrites the note with no new tool result).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Arms are compared on the tasks both completed. We report differences with 95\% bootstrap
intervals over tasks (10,000 resamples for single runs, 5,000 for pooled comparisons) and exact McNemar tests for task
success. Where several runs of the same policy exist (\llm{} run 1, \llm{} run 2 on 15 tasks, and the logging variant;
2.15 runs per task on average) we pool them per task before pairing.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Performance: choosing among proposals}\label{sec:select}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} give the headline for Clef. \sel{} passes \PBcpSel\% of checkpoints
against \PBcpLLM\% for the LLM ($\Delta$ \PBdiffSel{} points in task success, McNemar $p\PBpSel$). The noise floor is
wide: two runs of the same LLM policy differ on \PBnfDisc{} of 100 tasks ($\Delta$ \PBnfDiff{} points), so single-run
differences in task success of a few points carry no signal. Pooling the LLM's runs sharpens the checkpoint-level

Neither System One model moves the checkpoints. An early gain of pplx-decider-v1.2 on required orders failed a

\subsection{Deciding is not the bottleneck}\label{sec:timing}

Why does a better pick not show up in the outcome? On the fixed exam every decider makes far fewer key errors than
the LLM's own sample (0.17--0.27 against 0.48; \S\ref{sec:commit}), yet online the checkpoints do not move. Following
each required order along the LLM's own trajectories resolves the gap (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). Of the 94 steps at
which the LLM's sample skipped an available required order, 79\% are followed by that order later in the same run:
the step-level key mostly rewards ordering early, not ordering at all. Of all 118 required orders in 54 tasks, the LLM
places 69\%, 11\% are proposed at some step but never executed, and 19\% are never proposed by any sample. Selection
can at most recover the 11\% (on these trajectories, 4 more of 54 tasks would pass); the 19\% are bounded by
generation. The decision seat holds little of the LLM's error.

pplx-decider-v1.2 shows what a selector does with that margin. It recovers part of it (proposed but never executed:
4\% against 11\%, $-$7 points [$-$13, 0]) and gives about as much back on generation: it acts after less retrieval, and
4 points more of the required orders are never proposed ([$-$5, 13]). It places about as many as the LLM (+3 [$-$8,
12]). Its apparent gain on its first 76 tasks (+13 points on required-order checkpoints) failed a pre-registered
replication on the 24 tasks it had not run ($-$18 [$-$36, $-$2], against an LLM that already passes 84\% of them).

The exam cannot see any of this. Restricted to steps where the counterfactual is known (the LLM's sample takes no open
required order and a proposal places one the run never places), it holds only 11 questions in 9 tasks, too few to
rank deciders (take rates 0.64--0.91, intervals $\pm$0.2--0.3). Step-level answer keys inside an agent's own
trajectory measure decision \emph{style}; only whole runs rank deciders on outcome.

\subsection{Mechanism: a commitment bias}\label{sec:commit}

Why do the System One models shorten the episode? The fixed exam answers
on identical inputs (Tables~\ref{tab:exam} and \ref{tab:errors}). All six models agree with the LLM's own sample
about as often as a random pick would (21--27\% for five of them, 36\% for linne-oak-1.0; random \EXagreeRandom\%), and with each other
more often. They make far fewer key errors than the LLM's sample (0.17--0.27 against 0.48; every bootstrap interval
excludes 0), but the errors move rather than vanish: the LLM omits an available required order at 55\% of such steps
and the deciders at 2--18\%, while the deciders order when no required order is on offer at 52--71\% of steps against
the LLM's 35\%, and finish while a required order is open at 90--97\% of such steps against 80\%.

Two observations show that much of this is a preference over kinds of action rather than a reading of the patient.
First, a rule with no model, \emph{always take an ordering turn when one is offered}, reaches an error of 0.22 and
leaves no required order behind; on the order key it is better than every decider. Second, the deciders' low
redundancy is not evidence that they track what has been done: in 17--18 of the 19--22 questions where a decider
avoids the LLM's redundant option, it does so by choosing \textsc{finish} over a rewrite of the note. Table~\ref{tab:commit}
puts the preference in one number per kind: every model picks ordering turns 1.3--1.6$\times$ and finishing turns
1.3--1.4$\times$ as often as the LLM's own sample does, and retrieval turns 0.89--0.93$\times$. The same profile shows
online (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}: less retrieval before acting, fewer notes, shorter runs). We call it a
\emph{commitment bias}: System One deciders prefer turns that commit (order, write, finish) over turns that gather
information.

The bias is useful exactly where the LLM under-commits, and it costs elsewhere. pplx-decider-v1.2 online takes more
of the required orders that a proposal offers (offered but never executed: 4\% of required orders, against 11\% for
the LLM; difference $-$7 points [$-$13, 0]), but 4 points more are never proposed at all ([$-$5, 13]), so it places
about as many as the LLM (+3 [$-$8, 12]). Clef does not even gain the first part: on its own trajectories it places 6 points fewer of the required orders than
the LLM ([$-$15, 3]), and 8 points more of them are never proposed at all ([$-$1, 18]), plausibly because it moves to
action after less retrieval and the LLM then never thinks of them.

\paragraph{A prior and a misreading (blind test, pre-registered).} Does the bias read the patient at all? We asked
pplx-decider-v1.2 the whole exam again with the options but no state (same option letters; test P4, fixed before its
data existed). The pre-registered style hypothesis, that blind and informed picks agree on at least 60\% of questions
and the order and finish errors move by at most 0.05, is rejected: they agree on 41\% (random 22\%), and blind picks
match the kind of the informed pick barely more often than chance (91\% against 88\%; other deciders with the state:
95--98\%). The model reads the state. But the keys move in opposite directions (Table~\ref{tab:blind}). On orders,
the blind model is already far better than chance: about two thirds of the reduction from a random pick needs no
patient state, so the order preference is largely a prior over what the options look like. On redundancy, the state
does most of the work. On finishing, the state hurts: these are late steps where 73\% of the options are
\textsc{finish} and the LLM itself mostly wants to stop. Blind, the decider avoids finishing (below chance); with the
state, it finishes while a required order is open more often than chance and more often than the LLM's own sample.
The premature finishing is thus a misreading of the state, which agrees with, and strengthens, the LLM's own sense
that the case is done.

\subsection{Steering alone, the bias breaks the agent}\label{sec:steer}

\subsection{Boundaries: where a System One decider is reliable}\label{sec:boundaries}

On the keyed exam questions we split the benefit of executing the decider's pick instead of the LLM's sample (key
errors avoided per question) by what the decider and the LLM signal (Clef and pplx-decider-v1.2 shown; the other models behave alike).

\paragraph{Its confidence is informative.} Overrides where the decider prefers its pick to the LLM's sample by a
log-odds of at least 3 avoid 0.8--1.0 order-key errors per question and 0.6 redundancy errors; overrides below 1.5
avoid almost none. A margin keeps most of the benefit with far fewer overrides.

\paragraph{It helps where the reasoning model is unsure.} When only 1--2 of the LLM's five samples are semantically
the same decision as its first sample (same tools and key clinical arguments), the decider avoids 0.37--0.40 errors
per question; at 4 of 5 it avoids 0.11--0.17, and at 5 of 5 none. Order decisions sit almost entirely at 1 of 5. The
LLM's self-consistency and the decider's confidence are complementary signals for when to hand over.

\paragraph{Finishing is its blind spot.} Where the decider confidently overrules the LLM's sample toward
\textsc{finish} while a required order is still open, it is wrong in 4 of 5 cases (error change $-$0.75 to $-$0.80,
$n=4$--5), and every model finishes early more often than the LLM (Table~\ref{tab:errors}). In \tool{} the same
blindness appears as an agent that never stops writing.

\paragraph{Long states.} 85\% of keyed questions exceed the decider's 32k window and are read with shortened tool
results. The deciders' error is 0.03--0.07 on the questions read in full and 0.20--0.23 on shortened ones, against
0.44--0.47 and 0.51--0.52 for the LLM's sample; long, truncated trajectories are where the System One view is weakest
(confounded with late steps).

\subsection{System One plus the reasoning LLM}\label{sec:gate}

The boundaries suggest a narrower role. Let the LLM gather data, write the note and decide when to stop; consult the
System One model only where a proposal places an order, the decisions it gets right and the LLM defers. On the exam,
this \emph{orders-only} gate keeps the decider's order behaviour (pplx-decider-v1.2: omission 3\%) and leaves finishing
and redundancy at the LLM's level, while overruling the LLM on 11\% of steps instead of 73\%. Gating at every order or
finish step overrules on 19\%; a gate by log-odds margin or by the LLM's self-consistency does not lower the exam
error further under cross-validation, because the exam's redundancy key rewards finishing over a note rewrite and the
keys pull against each other. The exam cannot choose among these designs; whole runs must.

\paragraph{Pre-registered online tests.} We fixed these tests before their data existed. P2$'$ is running at the
time of writing, and we make no claim about the gate's online effect.
\textbf{P1} replicated pplx-decider-v1.2 in \sel{} on the 24 tasks it had not run; prediction: more required-order
checkpoints than the pooled LLM runs. It failed ($-$18 points [$-$36, $-$2], 16 tasks with such checkpoints;
\S\ref{sec:select}).
\textbf{P2$'$} runs the orders-only gate with pplx-decider-v1.2; prediction: the required-order gain of \sel{} (which
P1 has since failed to replicate) without its loss of notes (notes within the LLM's run-to-run noise, $-$0.12 [$-$0.40, 0.15]); documentation not lower; task
pass reported with its CI, claimed only if the CI excludes 0. The blind test P4 is done
(\S\ref{sec:commit}); it supports the design: the gate keeps the decider where its preference is a useful prior
(orders) and takes it away where reading the state misleads it (finishing).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Choosing among samples.} Majority voting over reasoning paths \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, trained
verifiers \citep{cobbe2021verifiers}, process reward models \citep{lightman2024verify} and LLM judges
\citep{zheng2023judge} all pick among sampled outputs. \sel{} applies best-of-$K$ selection at every step of an agent
loop, with a decision model as the selector; our gated variant uses the LLM's own self-consistency to decide when to
consult it.

\paragraph{System One decision models.} JEV introduced the typed-question interface \citep{typesafe2026jev}; Clef is
an open 27B model with a joint head over a Qwen3.8-27B backbone \citep{clef2026}. As judges they approach frontier
LLMs on verdicts that can be read off the input at a small fraction of the cost \citep{li2026jevjudge}; as next-action
selectors in general agents, most of their wrong actions follow a correct reading of the state \citep{li2026reading}.
We bring six of them into a clinical agent and characterise a shared bias.

\paragraph{Clinical agent benchmarks.} PhysicianBench grades long-horizon physician tasks in a live record system by
code and rubric \citep{physicianbench2026}. It has been run only with generative LLM agents.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Deciding is not the bottleneck.} Replacing the component that picks the next turn is the natural place to
put a fast decision model, and it is safe there, but it is not where a capable LLM agent loses its points. The LLM
mostly defers rather than omits, and what it never proposes no selector can choose. A decider that commits earlier
trades the few orders it recovers for orders the LLM would have proposed after more retrieval. Gains in clinical
agents of this kind must come from generation and information gathering, or from a decider that also controls when
the LLM keeps looking.

\paragraph{A commitment bias: a prior and a misreading.} The clearest regularity in our data is a preference over
kinds of action that six independently trained System One models share. It explains when they help (the LLM defers
orders it already proposes) and when they hurt (finishing early, writing instead of looking). The blind test splits
it: the preference for orders is largely a prior over the options, while the excess of premature finishing comes from
reading a state in which the work looks done, the same reading the LLM makes, held more firmly.

\paragraph{A division of labour.} The LLM is good at what the System One model cannot do: deciding what to look up,
how much evidence is enough, when to stop, and writing the note. The System One model is good at what the LLM does
unreliably: committing to a concrete order once it is on the table. The design that follows consults the System One
model at order steps only, and preferably where the LLM's samples disagree and the System One model is confident. The
ceiling above bounds what it can gain; its value may lie less in outcome than in cost and in a stable, inspectable
decision at the steps that commit.

\paragraph{Evaluating deciders inside agents.} Step-level keys built on an agent's own trajectory reward timing and
style. Their offline gains overstated the online effect by an order of magnitude here, and even an online gain on 76
tasks did not survive 24 more. Deciders should be ranked on whole runs, with several runs per arm and a held-out
replication, because the run-to-run noise of a single LLM policy spans $\pm$10 points of task success. A blind
control (the same questions without the state) separates what a decider reads from what it prefers.

\paragraph{Cost.} The System One decision is one forward pass, about \DECSels{} s per step on one GPU, and adds no
generated tokens. The cost of \sel{} lies in the five proposals (\TOKSel{} versus \TOKLLM{} generated tokens per
task); the orders-only gate needs proposals only where an order is on the table.

\section*{Limitations}

We study one LLM and one benchmark. Clef shares its backbone with the LLM; the five other System One models are
evaluated on the exam and, for one of them, online. Most online numbers come from one run per arm (the LLM has 2.15
runs per task when pooled), and the online test of the gated design is incomplete. The benchmark barely penalises
over-ordering, so a commitment bias costs little here and could cost more in practice. Our judge is the local LLM
rather than the leaderboard's, so absolute scores are not comparable with the leaderboard, while every arm shares the
judge. The System One models read a 32k-token view of states the LLM reads in full. Inpatient settings such as ClinEnv
\citep{clinenv2026} are left for future work.

\section*{Ethics and data}\label{sec:ethics}

PhysicianBench's records are real de-identified patients under a data use agreement. All models run locally, a socket
guard blocks any non-local connection from agent and grader processes, and trajectories and notes stay on
access-controlled storage outside version control. We report aggregates only.

\section{Decision interface}\label{app:interface}

\paragraph{Questions.} The System One model receives the state and one Choice question. For \sel{}: \emph{``The state
shows a clinical task assigned to an AI agent that works in an electronic health record through FHIR tools, followed
by the agent's actions and their results so far. Each option is a candidate next action for the agent. Choose the
candidate that best advances completing the task correctly, completely and safely.''} For \tool{} the second and third
sentences read: \emph{``Each option is a tool the agent can use for its next action, or finishing with a final answer.
Choose what the agent should do next to complete the task correctly, completely and safely.''} Options are the
rendered turns (\sel) or each tool's name with the first paragraph of its description, plus \textsc{finish} (\tool).

\paragraph{State.} The state is the task instruction followed by every step: the action, rendered as tool calls with
full arguments, and its results as the LLM saw them. It is fitted into the System One model's 32,768-token context
deterministically: tool results are kept in full, then shortened oldest-first to 800 characters, then omitted
oldest-first, each by the smallest number that fits. In the \sel{} run \DECSelshort\% of decisions saw shortened
results and \DECSelomit\% saw omitted ones.

\paragraph{Other System One models.} pplx-decider-v1.2 (\texttt{cce0f3f8}), d3 v3.1.0 (\texttt{cbff3a3c}),
linne-oak-1.0 (\texttt{20da7bf3}), amos-1-large (\texttt{d67e051c}) and decider-31b (\texttt{e12ddf20}) each run their
authors' own serving code on H100 GPUs, locally and offline. pplx-decider-v1.2 ships a library only, which we call on
its reference path. decider-31b is NVFP4, which an H100 runs through vLLM's weight-only FP4 path; its authors used
Blackwell GPUs. linne-oak-1.0 needs two GPUs for 32k-token inputs. Every model receives the state and question built
for Clef, with the same fitting rule, counted in its own tokenizer. Five exam questions whose options alone exceed the
32k window are unanswerable for every model.

\paragraph{The LLM.} Qwen3.8-27B with thinking on, temperature 1.0, top-$p$ 0.95, top-$k$ 20, at most 16,384 completion
tokens per call. Identical replicas serve all arms; each task is routed whole to the less loaded replica, independent
of the arm.
